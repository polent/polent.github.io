## Introduction

Karibuni! Today, the breeze from the Indian Ocean carries the scent of roasted spices and the promise of something truly comforting. This dish is my heart on a plate—a celebration of the velvety texture of njahi black beans, slow-simmered in freshly pressed coconut milk and kissed by the heat of berbere. It is a festival of the soil and the sea, designed to wrap you in warmth.

## Ingredients

* 2 cups njahi (black beans), soaked overnight and boiled until tender
* 2 cups fresh thick coconut milk, pressed from mature coconuts
* 1 large red onion, finely diced
* 3 cloves garlic, minced
* 1-inch piece of fresh ginger, grated
* 1 tablespoon berbere spice blend
* 1 teaspoon ground cardamom
* 1/2 teaspoon fenugreek seeds, toasted and crushed
* 2 medium sweet potatoes, peeled and cubed
* 1/2 cup roasted groundnuts, crushed into a smooth paste
* 2 tablespoons tamarind water (from soaked pulp)
* 1 bunch sukuma wiki (collard greens), finely shredded
* 2 bird's eye pilipili chiles, slit lengthwise
* Fresh kachumbari (diced tomatoes, onions, cilantro) for garnish
* 1 lime, cut into wedges
* Cold-pressed coconut oil

## Instructions

* Heat a tablespoon of coconut oil in a heavy clay pot or jiko over medium heat. Sauté the red onions until they are soft and translucent.
* Stir in the garlic, ginger, and pilipili chiles, followed by the berbere, cardamom, and crushed fenugreek. Let the spices bloom until the kitchen smells of the spice markets.
* Add the cubed sweet potatoes and the groundnut paste, stirring well to coat the vegetables in the aromatic oil.
* Pour in the thick coconut milk and tamarind water. Bring the mixture to a gentle simmer, being careful not to boil it too vigorously to prevent the coconut milk from curdling.
* Fold in the cooked njahi beans. Cover the pot and let it simmer for 20-25 minutes until the sweet potatoes are tender and the sauce has reduced into a thick, velvety gravy.
* Place the shredded sukuma wiki on top of the stew and cover for another 3 minutes, allowing the steam to wilt the greens while keeping them vibrant.
* Remove from heat and stir gently. Serve in deep earthenware bowls, topped with a generous spoonful of kachumbari and a fresh squeeze of lime juice.

As you scrape the last of the coconut cream from your bowl with a piece of warm chapati, remember that every ingredient tells a story of our trade winds and red earth. This dish is more than a meal; it is a piece of Mombasa. Enjoy the warmth, and let it nourish your soul until we cook together again.

*Nia*
