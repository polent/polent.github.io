## Introduction

Welcome to my kitchen where we celebrate the architecture of texture. Today, we are exploring the concept of 'Q'—that elusive, bouncy elasticity prized in Asian cuisines—fused with the rustic depth of wood-fired techniques. We are taking the humble mochi and treating it with the reverence of a hand-rolled pasta, bathing it in a complex, fermented umami jus that bridges the gap between old-world preservation and modern plant-based innovation.

## Ingredients

* 250g Kirimochi or fresh glutinous rice dough cut into bite-sized cubes
* 2 tablespoons fermented black beans (Douchi), rinsed and lightly bruised
* 1 tablespoon Shio Koji for marinating
* 200g heirloom cherry tomatoes, kept whole
* 2 cloves fermented black garlic, finely minced
* 1 tablespoon cold-pressed sesame oil
* 1 stalk lemongrass, white part only, bruised
* A handful of fresh Thai basil leaves
* 1 teaspoon charred chili oil
* Toasted white sesame seeds for garnish

## Instructions

* Begin by marinating the mochi cubes in Shio Koji for 15 minutes to break down the starches and add a hidden layer of sweetness.
* Heat a cast-iron skillet over a high flame, preferably over wood-fire or charcoal, until it begins to smoke.
* Toss the whole cherry tomatoes into the dry skillet, searing them until the skins blister and blacken slightly, then set aside.
* In the same skillet, add the sesame oil and bruised lemongrass, followed by the fermented black beans and black garlic. Sauté until the aroma is pungent and earthy.
* Add the mochi cubes to the pan. Allow them to sit undisturbed for 2 minutes to develop a crisp, golden crust on one side while maintaining a chewy heart.
* Return the charred tomatoes to the pan, pressing them gently so their juices mingle with the fermented beans to create a natural, thick jus.
* Remove the lemongrass stalk and fold in the fresh Thai basil leaves until just wilted.
* Finish with a drizzle of charred chili oil and a sprinkle of toasted sesame seeds before serving in warm ceramic bowls.

As you experience the resistance and release of each bite, remember that cooking is a bridge. This dish is a testament to how traditional fermentation and fire can elevate simple rice and beans into something transcendental. I hope this inspires you to look at plant-based textures through a new, more adventurous lens.

*Hiroshi*
