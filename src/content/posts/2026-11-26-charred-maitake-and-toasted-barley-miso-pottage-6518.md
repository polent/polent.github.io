## Introduction

In the quiet stillness of Kyoto's morning, rich soup is not created through heaviness, but through depth and patience. This bowl brings together charred maitake mushrooms grilled over binchotan, steeped in a slow-steeped kombu and roasted barley broth, finished with house-fermented red miso and freshly pressed silken soy milk. It is a warm meditation on shun, honoring earth and forest in their peak expression.

## Ingredients

* 200g fresh maitake mushrooms, gently torn into clusters
* 1 piece (10cm) Rishiri kombu
* 4 dried shiitake mushrooms, slow-steeped in cold spring water
* 30g whole pearled barley, dry-toasted until nutty and golden
* 400ml freshly made unsweetened rich soy milk (or homemade silken tofu curd base)
* 3 tbsp aged rustic red barley miso (mugi miso)
* 1 tsp aged dark tamari
* 1 small Japanese negi or spring onion, fine julienne cut using katsuramuki
* A few drops of cold-pressed toasted sesame oil
* Zest of fresh yuzu and a single sprig of kinome or purple shiso

## Instructions

* Wipe the Rishiri kombu with a clean damp cloth, then submerge it along with dried shiitake mushrooms in 800ml of cold water for at least 4 hours. Slowly bring to 60°C over gentle heat, discard kombu just before simmering, and strain the amber dashi.
* Lightly skewer the torn maitake clusters and sear over hot binchotan charcoal until edges are deeply blistered, fragrant, and slightly caramelized.
* In a heavy clay donabe, simmer the toasted barley in 300ml of the dashi until tender and creamy, then blend half of the barley dashi mixture with fresh soy milk until rich and velvet-smooth.
* Return the silky barley-soy base to the pot over low heat, whisking in the rustic red miso and aged tamari gently without letting it boil.
* Ladle the rich, steaming broth into a rustic, unglazed ceramic bowl.
* Arrange the charred maitake clusters gracefully atop the surface, finishing with crisp negi threads, a hint of fragrant yuzu zest, a whisper of sesame oil, and a single seasonal herb leaf.

Hold the bowl with both hands and breathe in the smoky charcoal and fermented barley before taking the first sip. In shojin ryori, finishing a warm broth is a gesture of gratitude to the season, returning warmth directly to the body and spirit.

*Hiroshi*
