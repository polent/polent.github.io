## Introduction

As the morning mist clings to the moss in our temple garden, I find myself drawn to the quiet transition of late spring into the early warmth of summer. Today, we celebrate the arrival of Shincha—the first tea harvest—and the fleeting sweetness of the loquat. This dessert is a study in textures: the ethereal silkiness of kuzu starch and the earthy, grounding dust of roasted kinako. It is a 'sprinkled' dish that mirrors the light rain falling over Kyoto, meant to be enjoyed in silence, appreciating the fleeting nature of the moment.

## Ingredients

* 50g Yoshino kuzu-ko (wild arrowroot starch)
* 250ml filtered water
* 1 tablespoon organic soy milk, freshly pressed
* 2 tablespoons roasted kinako (soybean powder)
* 1 teaspoon finely ground Shincha (new season green tea leaves)
* 2 tablespoons Kokuto (Okinawan black sugar) syrup
* 2 fresh loquats or early apricots, peeled and sliced using katsuramuki-style precision
* A tiny pinch of sea salt

## Instructions

* In a small heavy-bottomed pot, whisk together the kuzu-ko and water until the starch is completely dissolved and no lumps remain.
* Place the pot over low heat and add the soy milk. Stir constantly with a wooden spatula in a rhythmic, circular motion.
* As the mixture begins to thicken and turn translucent, increase your stirring speed. Continue for 5 minutes until the paste is thick, glossy, and elastic.
* Transfer the kuzu mixture into a dampened square wooden mold or glass container. Smooth the surface with a wet spatula and let it sit in a cool place (not the refrigerator) for 20 minutes to set while maintaining its delicate transparency.
* While the mochi sets, sift together the kinako, ground Shincha, and sea salt into a small bowl to create your sprinkling dust.
* Carefully unmold the kuzu-mochi onto a cutting board. Using a wet knife, cut into asymmetric, bite-sized cubes.
* Arrange three or four cubes on an imperfect ceramic plate. Place the sliced loquat gracefully beside them.
* Drizzle a thin stream of kokuto syrup over the mochi, then generously sprinkle the kinako-tea dust over the top, allowing it to fall like soft earth over the translucent peaks.

This dish is a meditation on balance. The bitterness of the tea, the saltiness of the kinako, and the deep molasses of the kokuto syrup exist in harmony, much like the stones in a dry garden. May this small offering bring a sense of calm to your day. Remember, the beauty lies not in perfection, but in the sincerity of the preparation. Peace to you.

*Hiroshi*
